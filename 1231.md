According to a document from **2025-12-31** (your uploaded draft), your paper **“When Waste Penalties Raise Profits: Disciplining Inventory Races under Competition”** asks a clean, high-stakes question: **can a per-unit penalty on *unsold* inventory reduce waste from availability-driven overstocking *without* killing firm performance?** 

Below is feedback in the style of a tough-but-constructive MS/MSOM referee (ignoring the missing conclusion, as requested).

## 1) What’s already strong (and very “MSOM/MS-EES friendly”)

**The core mechanism is crisp and novel enough to be interesting to a broad OM + sustainability audience.** You isolate a demand-capped “oversupply” regime and show a real “regulation-as-coordination-device” effect: below a threshold (\bar w(c)), increasing the waste penalty reduces inventory/waste while **industry profit rises** (and consumer surplus is not harmed)—then beyond the threshold, the instrument becomes distortionary by reducing sales. That structure is clean and explainable.

**The front-matter abstract is already in MSOM style and does a lot right.** You clearly state the inventory-race problem, the boundary logic, and the “why waste penalties differ from output/sales taxes” angle. This is exactly what editors like: a paper that can be summarized in 20 seconds without lying.

**You proactively add robustness dimensions that the obvious reviewer would ask for** (market-share motives, endogenous pricing, non-uniform demand), and you do a welfare/policy comparison against production and revenue taxes (Figure 8 etc.).

So the skeleton is good. The main upgrades are about (i) tightening claims/definitions so they survive adversarial reading, and (ii) making the paper’s punchline feel *inevitable* rather than “cute.”

## 2) Major revisions that would materially increase the chance at MS / MSOM

### A. Make the “oversupply” regime definition bulletproof (and empirically legible)

Right now, the oversupply regime is mathematically sharp: **total inventory (Y\ge M)** where (M) is the **upper bound** of demand, yielding “sales pinned at (E[D])” logic.

A referee will immediately think:

> “Do real markets have a known hard upper bound (M)? Isn’t demand unbounded? Isn’t this oversupply notion too strong?”

You can preempt this with two moves:

1. **Rename “oversupply” to something like “demand-capped / fully-covered market”** (you already sometimes say “demand-capped”) and explicitly interpret (M) as **a seasonal market potential cap** (population/installed-base/short selling season constraints). You already hint at this (“market potential… (Y_O=M)” boundary)—make it explicit early.

2. Add one short paragraph: **how a regulator/analyst would diagnose being in this regime empirically**. E.g., persistent (inventory ÷ sales) ratios, chronic unsold share (your apparel 40% example), capacity utilization, etc. You already motivate with these types of facts (semiconductors, fashion, solar PV), but you don’t yet translate that into **“this corresponds to (Y\ge M)”** in a way that feels operational.

The goal is to stop the knee-jerk “knife-edge assumption” objection before it starts.

---

### B. Pull the *intuition* up: “this is a Tullock contest in inventory clothing”

Your Proposition 1 is great, but the *reason* profits rise needs to feel unavoidable—not mysterious.

You already show in the oversupply regime that profit becomes:
$$ 
\pi_{i,o}(y_i,y_{-i})=\frac{M(r+w)}{2}\cdot \frac{y_i}{y_i+y_{-i}}-(c+w)y_i
$$
That’s basically a **contest success function** (share = investment / total investment), i.e., a Tullock contest in “inventory spend.”

Referee-friendly improvement:

* Add a **1-paragraph “contest interpretation”** right after the oversupply profit expression (or right after Proposition 1), explicitly saying: *inventory is strategic effort to win share of a fixed prize (expected demand); the waste penalty raises the cost of effort, reducing rent-seeking.*
* Then the “profits can rise” result becomes intuitive: you reduce wasteful rent-seeking in a prisoner’s dilemma.

Right now you explain “sales fixed → less waste → higher profit”, which is true but reads a bit like magic. Naming the contest structure makes it feel like established economics wearing a sustainability badge—exactly the vibe top journals like.

---

### C. Clarify what “waste” is in the model vs. in the motivating examples

Early on you write: unsold goods “must be discounted, scrapped, destroyed, or otherwise disposed of”. Your model maps “unsold” into **waste (W)** and applies a penalty (w).

A likely critique:

> “Discounting isn’t necessarily waste; and end-of-life waste (PV panel waste) is not the same as unsold inventory.”

Two fixes:

1. **Explicitly define your ‘waste’ concept as “units that do not deliver primary-market consumer value in the selling season,”** which can include destruction, forced donation, costly handling, severe markdown channels, etc. You already describe “policies effectively raise the private cost of producing units that remain unsold”—make the mapping explicit.

2. Consider adding (even briefly, maybe as an appendix robustness note) a **salvage/markdown value (s)** extension, or at least a remark: “(w) can be interpreted as the net cost of leftovers after salvage.” This will neutralize the “but firms salvage” objection without derailing the paper.

---

### D. Strengthen the non-uniform demand story (right now it’s a bit “numerical cameo”)

You do a non-uniform family $(F(D)=D^\alpha/M^\alpha)$ with $(\alpha\ge 1)$ (integer for tractability).

To a referee, that may read as: *“they tried two extra distributions and plots look similar.”* That’s not bad, but it’s not a theorem.

Two ways to make it stronger with minimal work:

* **State a small proposition/lemma:** In the oversupply regime (Y\ge M), the key comparative statics do **not depend on the shape of (F)**—only on (E[D]) (since sales are always (D) and shares are proportional). You essentially already have this structure in the oversupply expression. Make it explicit: it’s a robustness claim you can actually prove cleanly.
* Then position Section 4.3 as: “non-uniformity matters for the *transition* and for the no-oversupply regime, not for the win–win mechanism.”

That upgrade takes you from “we tried it” to “it’s structurally robust.”

---

### E. Welfare section: fix terminology and make the welfare accounting standard

In Appendix A you define consumer surplus as
$$CS=(v-r(Y))R + (\text{tax on firms}) - c_e W$$
and then define social welfare as $SW=\Pi+CS$.

A referee will likely say:

* taxes are transfers, not surplus (unless you're explicitly rebating lump-sum),
* and the externality term is not “consumer surplus.”

Simple fix that improves credibility:

* Rename your current CS to something like **“consumer net utility”** or explicitly say **“we assume tax revenues are rebated lump-sum to consumers”** (you do say this in Appendix D’s tax comparison setup).
* Present **standard decomposition** in the main text: consumer surplus (classic), firm profit, minus external damage, plus transfers (cancel).
  This is mostly a presentation cleanup, but it prevents conceptual nitpicks from derailing the paper.

---

### F. Policy comparison: bring the “productive vs unproductive units” framing forward

Your best *policy-design* sentence is buried near Figure 8: the difference is whether policy prices **productive units (sold output)** or **unproductive units (unsold inventory)**. That’s a killer framing. It’s also *exactly* the kind of “big idea” that Management Science likes.

I’d elevate this to:

* the abstract (1 sentence),
* the introduction “three insights” paragraph, and
* the beginning of Section 5.

Because it’s the conceptual hook that explains why waste penalties can be politically salient and not just another tax instrument.

---

### G. Market-share motive section: ensure it strengthens (not distracts)

Your market-share extension is motivated with “land-grab dynamics” and Table 1 examples. This is interesting, but it risks feeling like a *different paper* if the reader thinks those examples are not inventory-waste contexts.

Two refinements:

1. In Section 4.1, explicitly connect “market share weight (\lambda)” to **availability competition**: proportional rationing turns inventory into a direct lever on share. That’s your bridge—make it explicit in the first paragraph.
2. Curate Table 1 to keep at least a couple examples that scream “physical overproduction/waste” (fast fashion, electronics, etc.), since your paper’s sustainability angle is waste.

This keeps the extension as a reinforcement: “if firms are even more share-obsessed, the inventory race is worse and the policy value is larger.”

---

### H. Make calibration guidance more “implementable”

You already have the comparative statics: the threshold (\bar w(c)) rises with competition intensity (n), expanding the range where oversupply happens. That’s valuable for policymakers.

A very MS/MSOM-friendly enhancement is: **one short “how to set w” paragraph**:

* If you want “no harm to sales” in the oversupply regime, set (w) **below** (\bar w(c)).
* If your goal is to eliminate demand-capped overproduction (hit the boundary (Y=M)), set (w \approx \bar w(c)) (your “knife-edge boundary”).
* Discuss what observables might help bound (\bar w(c)): markup proxies, number of active competitors, persistent inventory glut indicators, etc.

This turns the result from “nice theorem” into “policy knob with a dashboard,” which sustainability-area editors love.
