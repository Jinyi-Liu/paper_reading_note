## Referee Report — MSOM-2025-1006 

**Title:** *Designing Compensation Contracts for Time-Inconsistent Employees: The Role of Sophistication and Naivety in Goal-Oriented Incentives* 

**Recommendation:** **Reject**

### 1. Summary

This paper studies goal-based bonus contracts for employees with present-biased (time-inconsistent) preferences, distinguishing between **sophisticated** employees who anticipate their self-control problem and **naive** employees who are overly optimistic about future behavior. Using a **bilevel Stackelberg** framework, the authors characterize the firm’s optimal goal/bonus contract and show that present bias induces procrastination; in high-reservation-utility environments it can paradoxically increase the sophisticated employee’s net utility by forcing more favorable terms. For naive employees under belief symmetry, the model predicts “accept-then-abandon” behavior (mid-project quitting) and proposes two remedies: **naivety-responsive** contracts (ex post adjustment) and **naivety-embedded** contracts (ex ante flexible design), comparing their profit and welfare implications.

### 2. Strengths

1. **Relevant and timely behavioral contracting question.** The paper addresses procrastination, abandonment, and goal-based incentives—phenomena that appear in project work, R&D, sales quotas, and milestone compensation.

2. **Clear tractable structure with closed-form characterizations.** The baseline model yields clean effort allocation and contract regions, which can be pedagogically valuable.

3. **Explicit treatment of naivety and “wasted effort” in an employment contracting setting.** The “accept-then-abandon” outcome is interesting and potentially publishable, especially if the paper can strengthen the institutional realism and robustness of this mechanism.

4. **Contracting remedies are conceptually appealing.** Separating ex post adaptation vs ex ante design is a nice organizing framework for managerial implications.

### 3. Major Concerns

#### 3.1. Contract adjustment at the intermediate stage: commitment vs renegotiation

A central part of the contribution is the “naivety-responsive” contract, where the firm—after observing that the employee would abandon—**reduces the target or increases the bonus at the intermediate stage**.

This raises a fundamental issue: **what is the contracting/commitment environment?**

* If the firm is free to revise the contract at (t=1), then the original (t=0) contract is not a commitment device; it becomes the opening move of a dynamic renegotiation game.
* If the employee anticipates that the firm will “rescue” the contract at (t=1), then her (t=0) acceptance and early effort choices may change. The model currently treats the (t=1) adjustment as a kind of managerial intervention that does not feed back into (t=0) beliefs/strategies, but in many settings it would.

#### 3.2. The quitting payoff at (t=1) is set to zero; this is both unrealistic and load-bearing

In the model, if the employee quits midway, she “leaves with nothing.” That assumption is pivotal for generating severe inefficiency and for welfare comparisons.

In practice, a mid-project quitter often has:

* an outside option at (t=1) (maybe lower than at (t=0), but rarely exactly zero),
* and the firm often retains some salvage value from partial progress (documentation, prototypes, partial deliverables), even if the final milestone is missed.

With a nonzero (t=1) outside option or a positive salvage value, several results could shift:

* the region where abandonment happens,
* the relative attractiveness of lowering targets vs increasing bonus,
* and especially the welfare ranking between “embedded” and “responsive” contracts.

#### 3.3. Information and observability: how does the firm “discover naivety”?

The responsive contract relies on the firm becoming aware of the employee’s true present bias “through ex post observation of behavioral inconsistencies.”

But in your setup, intra-project effort allocation is explicitly **not contractible** and seems largely **unobservable** (and you motivate this with private knowledge about activities). If the firm cannot observe effort directly, then:

* what exactly is observed at (t=1)?
* how does observation map into learning about the parameter ($\beta$) (or even learning that the employee is naive)?

If the only observable event is “the employee wants to quit,” then the firm learns a *decision*, not a *parameter*, and the natural object is not “learning the true ($\beta$)” but “updating the probability of abandonment” or “learning that ($\beta$) is below a threshold.”

#### 3.4. Implementability of the naivety-embedded contract: hidden future goal reduction is delicate

In the embedded contract, the firm sets an ambitious goal initially and later can reduce it to a lower target that is “unobserved by the fully naive employee until (t=1).” This assumption needs much stronger justification.

In many real organizations:

* employees know policies about target resets, performance reviews, and renegotiations,
* or can infer them from experience and social information,
* and the firm typically cannot credibly hide future target reductions (and if it could, it raises transparency/fairness concerns that may affect acceptance and morale).

#### 3.5. Functional form dependence and scope of generality

The model uses deterministic linear output and quadratic effort cost. This gives clean closed forms, but some conclusions might be knife-edge.

In particular:

* the “lower the target vs increase the bonus” ranking,
* the sharp region boundaries,
* and the “paradox” about employee utility in high outside-option environments,

may depend on these choices.

**What I want to see in a revision:**

* A short section (or appendix) stating which results are structural (expected to hold with general convex costs / concave production) and which rely on the quadratic-linear specification.
* Ideally, one robustness check: alternative convex cost curvature or mild nonlinearity in output.

#### 3.6. Welfare comparisons need a clearer normative baseline

The paper discusses “social welfare” as firm profit + employee net utility, and concludes that embedded contracts can maximize firm profit at the cost of employee utility and social welfare.

However, with present bias and naivety, welfare analysis is tricky:

* Which utility is used—ex post realized utility, ex ante perceived utility, or a “planner” utility?
* Does welfare incorporate the employee’s outside option and opportunity costs consistently across scenarios (especially with abandonment)?

### 4. Overall Recommendation

The paper addresses an important and interesting intersection of behavioral economics and operations incentive design. The sophisticated/naive split and the focus on mid-project abandonment are potentially valuable contributions for MSOM. However, several of the strongest claims currently rest on assumptions that are either (i) institutionally unclear (commitment/renegotiation; hidden goal adjustment), or (ii) economically load-bearing and arguably unrealistic (zero quitting outside option; zero salvage value; strong observability of behavioral “inconsistency”). I therefore recommend **Reject**.
